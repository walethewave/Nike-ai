striking illustration of a pattern this line of work keeps surfacing: raw
model scale does not substitute for Yor\`ub\'a-specific fine-tuning, even
against a much smaller fine-tuned competitor. We select
\citet{whisper-small-yoruba} for the deployed pipeline (\S\ref{sec:methodology})
on this basis. Tone-sensitive scoring is uniformly worse than tone-agnostic
scoring for every system --- a 13.1-point WER gap for the winning model

that tone marking materially affects reported Yor\`ub\'a ASR quality; the
ranking across systems does not change between the two scoring modes in
our results, but the \emph{magnitude} of every system's error rate does,
which matters for anyone citing a single WER number without specifying
which mode it was computed under.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Attempt: A Negative Result}
\label{sec:finetune-results}
Motivated by \S\ref{sec:data}'s permissively-licensed corpus, we further
fine-tuned \citet{whisper-small-yoruba} itself on 150 held-in utterances
(disjoint from a 30-utterance held-out evaluation set) drawn from Common
Voice~17 and FLEURS, to test whether additional training on top of an
already-fine-tuned checkpoint could close more of the remaining gap. An
initial attempt (under one epoch, learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, 5-step
warmup) made held-out WER \emph{worse} (57.5\% $\to$ 62.5\%), which we
traced to insufficient training combined with too aggressive a learning
rate for so little data. A corrected recipe (3 full epochs, learning rate
$5\times10^{-6}$, 10\% warmup ratio) showed a real recovery trend across
epochs (57.5\% $\to$ 64.6\% $\to$ 60.4\% $\to$ 55.2\% on a fixed
per-epoch tracking subset) but still landed slightly worse than the
zero-shot baseline on the full held-out set (57.5\% $\to$ 61.4\%,
$\Delta{=}{+}3.9$ points) at the point training stopped. We attempted to
extend training to 5 epochs to test whether the trend would continue past
this point, but this failed twice for infrastructure reasons specific to
our hardware (Apple Silicon / MPS): once from an out-of-memory crash 71\%
through training, and once, after enabling gradient checkpointing as the
standard memory mitigation, from a distinct PyTorch reentrant-checkpointing
crash incompatible with Whisper's decoder caching that we did not
further diagnose. We report this honestly as an unresolved negative
result rather than omitting it: with 150 training utterances and $\leq$3
epochs on this hardware, further fine-tuning an already-fine-tuned
Yor\`ub\'a Whisper checkpoint did not improve held-out WER, though the
per-epoch trajectory suggests it might with either more data, more epochs
on stronger hardware, or both --- a claim we did not verify. (While
building the training harness we also found and fixed a reproducibility
bug worth noting for other practitioners: seeding Python's \texttt{random}
module for the train/eval split is not sufficient for reproducible
training runs, since it leaves PyTorch's own RNG --- governing batch
shuffling and dropout --- unseeded; two "identical-configuration" runs
produced different trajectories until \texttt{torch.manual\_seed()} was
also set.)

\subsection{Yor\`ub\'a-Numeral Parsing: An LLM Failure Mode and Its Fix}
\label{sec:numeral-results}
Independent of ASR quality, we found that the LLM stage of our pipeline
(Claude Haiku~4.5, used for dialogue and, in our finance-tracking
deployment, structured amount extraction) unreliably parses Yor\`ub\'a
number words even when given clean, correctly-transcribed text as input.
A systematic test of 14 real Yor\`ub\'a number words, each substituted into
a fixed carrier sentence and passed through the live model, found only
5/14 (36\%) parsed to the correct numeric value. The failure pattern is
sharp rather than uniformly noisy: the model is reliable on small units
spoken alone (1--10) and on simple round-thousand multiples, but the
entire tens/hundreds range is broken, including Yor\`ub\'a's irregular
base-20 subtractive forms (e.g.\ \emph{\`a\'ad\'ota}, literally
"ten-short-of-sixty," for 50). Errors are also non-deterministic: the
single sentence that first surfaced this issue was re-run three times at
identical settings and produced three \emph{different} wrong answers
across the three calls. This matters specifically because the failure is
silent --- there is no low-confidence signal, malformed output, or refusal
that would flag the extracted number as untrustworthy; the model states a
specific, plausible-looking wrong number with the same confidence as a
correct one, which is the worst failure mode for a value written directly
to a financial ledger.

We built and verified a fix: a small, fully deterministic Yor\`ub\'a-number
parser (rule-based, no model inference) that independently parses the same
text and cross-checks the LLM's extracted amount, auto-correcting on
disagreement and surfacing the correction transparently in the system's
reply rather than silently overwriting the value. Verified live against
the same production model and endpoint, the guardrail achieved 13/13
correct, including full recovery of the original failing case and every
one of the 8 words that had been wrong in the systematic test above. Two
implementation pitfalls surfaced while building it are worth noting as
concrete evidence of how easy Yor\`ub\'a NLP is to get subtly wrong even
when actively trying to fix something: (1) Yor\`ub\'a tone-marked
underdot vowels (e.g.\ \emph{\'{\d{e}}}, an underdotted \emph{\d{e}} with
an acute tone mark) are not single Unicode
codepoints even after NFC normalization --- they decompose into a base
letter plus a separate combining diacritic --- so a na\"ive per-codepoint
tone-stripping pass silently leaves some tone marks in place; and (2) an
initial "if the word after the thousand-marker is unrecognized, default to
a bare thousand" fallback was itself an instance of the exact failure mode
under investigation --- a confident, silent wrong answer --- and had to be
replaced with a fallback that refuses to guess.

Deploying this guardrail is not sufficient on its own, however: a
full-pipeline (ASR $\to$ LLM $\to$ TTS $\to$ ASR-again, in our
voice-message-replay evaluation setup) diagnostic found that upstream
text-to-speech non-determinism can itself corrupt the number word before
the guardrail ever sees it (\S\ref{sec:limitations}), and that an unset
LLM temperature parameter (defaulting to the API's 1.0 for what should be
a deterministic extraction task) compounds the same silent-wrong-answer
risk independently of numeral parsing specifically. Fixing all three
issues together --- an opt-in deterministic TTS seed, \texttt{temperature=0}
on the extraction call, and the guardrail's vocabulary --- brought
repeated end-to-end runs of the same input from 0/6 correct (before this
investigation) to 3/3 correct, with the correction visibly surfaced in the
system's own reply. We consider this three-bug diagnostic chain, more than
any single fix in isolation, the paper's clearest illustration of why a
cascaded pipeline with deterministic guardrails at each silent-failure
point is a more defensible design for user-facing deployment than trusting
a single large model end-to-end, however capable that model is on other
axes.
